K-Means Clustering

Concepts behind unsupervised learning, clustering, and the K-Means algorithm assigned to this group, applied here to segment students by study habits and academic performance.

What is Unsupervised Learning?

Unsupervised learning is a type of machine learning where the model learns patterns from data that has no labels or target variable. Unlike supervised algorithms such as Logistic Regression or Decision Trees, there is no "correct answer" to predict for each record.

Instead, the goal is to discover hidden structure in the data itself — for example, grouping similar records together — without ever being told in advance what those groups should look like.

What is Clustering?

Clustering is the most common unsupervised learning task. It groups records so that records in the same cluster are more similar to each other than to records in other clusters. Similarity between two records is usually measured with the Euclidean distance between their numerical variables: the smaller the distance, the more similar the records are.

K-Means is the most widely used clustering algorithm. The "K" refers to the number of clusters the algorithm must find, which is chosen beforehand.

How the algorithm works

K-Means finds clusters by repeatedly assigning points to the nearest center (centroid) and then recalculating those centers, until the assignments stop changing significantly.

  1. Initialize centroids: choose K starting points (one per cluster).
  2. Assign points: each record joins the cluster of its nearest centroid, measured by Euclidean distance.
  3. Recompute centroids: each centroid moves to the average position (mean) of all the points assigned to it.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the centroids stop moving significantly, or a maximum number of iterations is reached.

Types of problems it can solve

K-Means and clustering in general are versatile and can be used for:

Customer / user segmentation Anomaly detection Image compression Document / topic grouping

In this project, K-Means is used for student segmentation: grouping students into three performance segments based on their weekly study hours and final grade.

Main characteristics

  • Requires the number of clusters (K) to be chosen beforehand, usually through domain knowledge or methods like the elbow curve or the silhouette score.
  • Works only with numerical variables, since it relies on computing distances and averages.
  • The final result depends on the initial centroids chosen; different starting points can lead to different clusters.
  • Convergence is measured by tracking the within-cluster variance, which should decrease (or stabilize) with each iteration.

Advantages

  • Simple to understand and implement.
  • Scales well to large datasets compared to other clustering methods.
  • Produces clusters that are easy to interpret through their centroid coordinates.
  • Fast to converge in most practical cases.

Limitations

  • The number of clusters (K) must be chosen in advance.
  • Sensitive to the initial centroids; a poor choice can lead to a suboptimal solution.
  • Assumes clusters are roughly round and similarly sized, which is not always true.
  • Sensitive to the scale of the variables, so features often need to be normalized first.

Real-world example

Retail companies commonly use K-Means to segment customers by income and spending behavior, so they can design targeted marketing campaigns for each group. This project applies the same idea to education: instead of segmenting customers, K-Means groups students by weekly study hours and final grade into at-risk, average, and high-achieving segments, so an instructor could decide where to focus support.
